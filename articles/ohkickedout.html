Oh She's Getting Kicked Out! - The Debate

By Mariana Alara

Open TikTok for just a few minutes and you'll likely stumble across a woman adjusting a crisp white button-down, slipping on a pair of rectangular glasses, and finishing her outfit with a structured blazer and pointed heels. The Office Siren aesthetic has quickly become one of Gen Z's defining fashion trends.

Behind the viral videos lies a much bigger conversation. The Office Siren has become a cultural flashpoint, prompting discussions about confidence, professionalism, beauty standards, and the expectations placed on women in professional spaces. In many ways, the debate says less about clothes and more about the society interpreting them.

Although the Office Siren feels distinctly modern, the aesthetic borrows heavily from the past.

Its sharp tailoring recalls the power dressing of the 1980s, when women entering corporate environments adopted structured suits and broad shoulders to command authority in male- dominated workplaces. The sleek silhouettes of the late 1990s and early 2000s ( popularized though films, television, and fashion editorials) also echo throughout the trend.

Today's version belongs to social media.

Instead of simply dressing for the office, creators are dressing for an audience. Outfit breakdowns, "Get Ready With Me" videos, and office vlogs have transformed workwear into a lifestyle aesthetic, where ambition is communicated as much through appearance.

Fashion publications such as Business of Fashion have noted that nostalgia plays a significant role in contemporary trends, with younger generations continuously reinterpreting styles from previous decades. The Office Siren is no exception. Rather than inventing something entirely new, it repackages familiar corporate fashion.

For supporters, the Office Siren represents something surprisingly simple: freedom.

For years, women have navigated contradictory expectations in professional spaces. They are encouraged to appear polished but not vain, confident but not intimidating, feminine but not distracting. The Office Siren rejects the assumption that professionalism requires suppressing personal style.

Instead, it argues that ambition and femininity can exist together. Many women describe dressing intentionally as an act of confidence. A tailored blazer or structured dress doesn't necessarily change a person's abilities, but it can change how they feel walking into a meeting or beginning the workday.

Psychologists Hajo Adam and Adam Galinsky describe this phenomenon as enclothed cognition—the idea that clothing can influence psychological processes because of the symbolic meaning attached to what we wear. Their research suggests that clothing affects not only how others perceive us but also how we perceive ourselves. This helps explain why the Office Siren resonates with so many young professionals. It’s about feeling prepared, capable and self assured.

Yet the same qualities that attract supporters have also made the Office Siren controversial.

Critics argue that the trend romanticizes a version of corporate life that rarely reflects reality.

The glamorous office shown online often overlooks the everyday pressures of deadlines, long commutes, and workplace politics.

In her commentary on the Office Siren, Vogue writer Hannah Jackson argues that it would be naïve to assume the aesthetic exists outside the influence of patriarchy. Even when embraced as a form of empowerment, the trend still operates within a culture that frequently evaluates women through appearance before competence.

This criticism does not suggest that women should avoid dressing fashionably. Instead, it asks a more complicated question:

Why do women's clothes continue to provoke conversations about professionalism that men's rarely do?

It is a question without an easy answer.

Like every internet aesthetic, the Office Siren will eventually fade.

Another trend will emerge, another wardrobe will dominate our feeds, and another generation will redefine workplace fashion in its own image.

But the questions raised by the Office Siren are unlikely to disappear.

Who defines professionalism?

Can fashion be empowering without becoming performative?

Should femininity still be considered incompatible with authority?

The Office Siren offers no definitive answers.

Instead, it reminds us that clothing has always been more than fabric stitched together. It is a reflection of culture, ambition, identity, and the expectations society continues to place on those who wear it.
